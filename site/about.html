The Ghana Platinum Jubilee.

On 6 March 1957 the Black Star rose over an independent Ghana. On 6 March 2027 the nation reaches seventy years, its Platinum Jubilee, and everyone who loves Ghana, from across the world, will celebrate it in style, with three days in London. Meet the founders.

1957 · 2027 · Seventy years

A Platinum Jubilee for the Motherland.

At midnight on 6 March 1957, tens of thousands stood under the warm night sky of Accra and waited.

Then Kwame Nkrumah spoke the words a continent had waited a lifetime to hear: “Ghana, your beloved country, is free forever.” The crowds celebrated through the night. That morning the Duchess of Kent, representing the Queen, read her message to the new nation: “The hopes of many, especially in Africa, hang on your endeavours.”

Ghana carried those hopes. Ghana carries them still.

Continue the story

Seventy years on, the children of that midnight are everywhere, from London and Milton Keynes to Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow, Cardiff, Belfast and Dublin, from Amsterdam and Hamburg to New York, Houston and Toronto, and home in Accra and Kumasi. Our parents arrived with a suitcase, a winter coat that was never quite warm enough and an extraordinary amount of courage. They worked the night shifts, studied after long days, filled the church halls on Sunday and sent money home on Friday. They built careers, businesses and families, and they raised us to know exactly where we come from.

We have never forgotten home.

Some of us were born in Kumasi, some in Lewisham, in Manchester or in Milton Keynes. Some of us speak Twi, Ga, Ewe, Fante or Dagbani. Some of us only understand it when our mothers are angry.

Every one of us carries a Ghana of our own: waakye on a Saturday morning, a grandmother calling us by our day name, the first highlife record at a family party, the cousin who taught us to dance Azonto. And every one of us knows the words of that midnight.

That is why our symbol is Akoko Nan, the leg of the hen. An Akan proverb says the hen treads on her chicks, but she does not kill them. It is the love of a mother who shelters and corrects, firm and gentle at once, and never lets go. Its two curls are the chicks held close, its crescent the line that shields them, and its point faces forward, because a mother meets danger before it reaches her children. Gold for the Gold Coast, green for the land.

Ghana is ours, and we are Ghana. The Motherland is us.

Seventy years is a platinum jubilee. In June 2022 Britain showed the world how such a milestone is honoured. For Queen Elizabeth II, the first British monarch to reign for seventy years, the nation gave a four day bank holiday weekend, from 2 to 5 June. More than 3,500 beacons blazed across the UK and the Commonwealth. A Service of Thanksgiving was held in the City of London. Streets became banqueting halls, with more than 85,000 Big Jubilee Lunches, and some 10,000 people carried the Platinum Jubilee Pageant past Buckingham Palace.

Now it is the turn of Ghana.

The two stories were always joined. Elizabeth II came to the throne in 1952. Five years into her reign, Ghana became independent with her as Queen of Ghana, until the nation declared itself a Republic on 1 July 1960. In November 1961 she came to Accra as the guest of President Kwame Nkrumah, and the two danced together at the farewell ball, a moment still remembered in both countries.

On 6 March 2027 Ghana reaches a Platinum Jubilee of its own: seventy years as a sovereign nation. It deserves the same ceremony, the same pride and the same joyful noise. So guests from across the UK, Europe, the Americas and Ghana will gather at Banking Hall, London, for Ghana at 70 UK, The Platinum Jubilee Celebrations.

Britain gave the Queen four days. We give Ghana three.

A platinum jubilee asks two questions: what have these seventy years given us, and what will we give the next seventy? We gather to honour the Ghana we came from, to shape the one our children will inherit, and to lift one another while we do it.

For those who came before us. For those who walk beside us. For those who will follow.

Three days. One people. One Platinum Jubilee.

The meaning

What is a platinum jubilee?

A jubilee is a great anniversary, kept with ceremony, thanksgiving and joy. Each has its own name, and seventy years has the finest of all: platinum.

  4. Sapphire65 years2022

The meaning, in full

The sound of the horn

The word jubilee comes from the ancient Hebrew yobel, the horn of a ram. In the old law, a horn was sounded every fiftieth year to open a year of restoration: land returned to its families, debts were set aside, and those held in bondage were released.

“Proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof.”Leviticus 25:10

For a nation that lit the way to independence for a continent, no word could be more fitting. A jubilee was always a celebration of liberty, of homecoming and of a people restored to themselves.

What a platinum jubilee asks of us

  1. Give thanks.For seventy years of nationhood, and for everyone who paid for it.
  2. Remember.The names, the sacrifices and the songs, so that our children know them by heart.
  3. Come together.Every region, every faith and every generation, in one hall, across three days.
  4. Look ahead.To the Ghana of the next seventy years, which we will help to build.

Our Platinum Jubilee

Seventy years ago a people stood together at midnight and claimed their future. Seventy years on, we honour every generation that carried the Black Star forward: the farmers and the teachers, the nurses and the soldiers, the market women and the scholars, and the mothers and fathers who crossed the sea so that their children could stand tall in Britain.

This is our seventy years of service, of sacrifice and of faith. It deserves its own beacon, its own feast and its own pageant, in the heart of London, in our finest cloth.

Sound the horn. Light the beacon. Lay the table.

Who we are

We build rooms where everyone is honoured.

Ghana at 70 UK is made by two joint founders, Asantewaa and Adwoa, who have spent their working lives bringing the diaspora together, across the UK, in Accra and everywhere in between.

Every year on 6 March, Ghana celebrates the night it stood before the world and lit the way for a continent. For the seventieth, we are bringing that night to London for the whole world: three days, one great hall, and a welcome for every family of the Motherland, wherever on earth they live.

We are not a committee and we are not a brand. We are daughters of the Motherland who believe every guest deserves the finest room in the country, and the warmest welcome in it.

The symbol, explained
The symbol

The mother who protects.

Our symbol is Akoko Nan, an Adinkra symbol carried by an Akan proverb: Akoko nan tia ba, na enkum ba. The hen treads on her chicks, but she does not kill them.

It is the love of a mother who shelters her children and corrects them, but never harms them. Firm and gentle at once. At seventy, Ghana is that mother. Her children are everywhere, in London and Manchester, in Hamburg and Amsterdam, in New York and at home in Accra, and however far we travel, she keeps us close.

  1. The two curlsThe chicks, held close, one above and one below.
  2. The crescentThe strong outer line that shields them.
  3. The pointFacing forward. A mother meets danger before it reaches her children.
  4. Gold and greenGold for the Gold Coast, the name the land carried before 1957. Green for the land of the Motherland.

Why a mother. The Platinum Jubilee opens on Independence Eve and closes on Mothering Sunday: the celebration of a nation that ends with a celebration of the women who carried it. No other symbol would do.

The flag and the symbol. The flag belongs to the nation. The symbol belongs to the family. Red, gold and green fly for Ghana. Gold and green, with Akoko Nan, mark this gathering of her children.

The wordmark. GHANA AT 70 UK is set in wide, classical capitals beneath the symbol, with the line The Platinum Jubilee Celebrations, so the name reads with the calm of an engraved invitation. Akoko Nan is also cast as the Jubilee pin, in solid brass plated in 24 carat gold.

Her story, in full
The woman who designed the flag

Theodosia Okoh.

Every flag raised at Ghana at 70 UK began on the drawing board of one woman. Theodosia Salome Okoh, an artist trained at Achimota School, designed the flag of Ghana, flown for the first time at independence on 6 March 1957.

Born in Effiduase on 13 June 1922, she answered the call for a flag for the new nation, and her design was adopted by Kwame Nkrumah. She went on to become the first woman to chair the Ghana Hockey Association, led the sport for more than twenty years, and saw Ghana reach its first Hockey World Cup and Olympic Games. The National Hockey Stadium in Accra bears her name. She died in 2015, aged 92, and was honoured with a state funeral.

Before 1957

Our diaspora story.

Long before independence, our people were crossing oceans to Britain: princes and priests, scholars and sprinters, lawyers, soldiers and the students who would lead a nation to independence. These are some of those who came before us.

Read the ten stories
  1. 1555

    Shama to London

    Five men from Shama, on the Gold Coast, come to London to learn English for the gold trade. Nearly five centuries of Ghanaians in Britain begin.

  2. 1749

    The Royal African

    William Ansah Sessarakoo of Anomabu, sold into slavery and freed, arrives in London as a guest of the Earl of Halifax. His portrait, painted and engraved in London, survives to this day.

  3. 1754

    A boy from Cape Coast

    Philip Quaque leaves Cape Coast to study in England. In 1765 he becomes the first African ordained in the Church of England.

  4. 1882

    The fastest man in Britain

    Arthur Wharton of Jamestown, Accra, arrives in England. In 1886 he equals the 100 yards world record, then becomes the first Black professional footballer in the world.

  5. 1896

    A lawyer for a nation

    J. E. Casely Hayford is called to the Bar in London, and later writes one of the first novels in English by an African.

  6. 1925

    Africa in Camden

    The West African Students Union is founded in London, with J. B. Danquah its first president. Its Africa House becomes a home from home.

  7. 1927

    The doctor of philosophy

    J. B. Danquah becomes the first West African to earn a doctorate from a British university.

  8. 1945

    Burghley Road, Kentish Town

    Kwame Nkrumah rents a room at 60 Burghley Road, Kentish Town, and helps organise the Fifth Pan African Congress. A blue plaque marks the house.

  9. 1948

    The soldiers who came home

    Veterans of the Gold Coast Regiment, marching for promised benefits, are fired on in Accra. Their deaths set the nation on the road to freedom.

  10. 1957

    Independence, and the journey continues

    Ghana is independent, under a flag of red, gold and green designed by Theodosia Okoh. At the 2021 census, 132,577 people born in Ghana lived in England and Wales.

All ten stories stand on the Heritage Wall at Banking Hall across the three days, so every guest walks past the people who came before them. Partner a story.

Sources: London Museum, English Heritage, the Inner Temple, the Office for National Statistics 2021 census and published histories.
